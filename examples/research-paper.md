# The Illusion of Explanatory Depth: Five Levels of Explanation

This annotated application of the [guide](../five-level-explanation-guide.md) explains one reported study, not the entire research literature on explanation or learning.

## Source and Scope

- **Paper:** Leonid Rozenblit and Frank Keil (2002), *The misunderstood limits of folk science: an illusion of explanatory depth*, *Cognitive Science*, 26(5), 521-562. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1207/s15516709cog2605_1); [accessible author manuscript](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3062901/).
- **Inspected:** the abstract, the introduction's account of explanatory overconfidence, and Study 1's methods, results, and discussion, Sections 2.1.1-2.1.3.
- **Focus:** how attempting a detailed explanation relates to participants' ratings of their own understanding in Study 1.
- **Limits:** no participant-level data, supplemental stimulus packet, or original CD-ROM materials were obtained; no independent reanalysis or replication was performed. Later studies are outside this example's evidential scope. No comparison sufficient to establish historical priority was made.

## The Through-Line

Feeling that you understand a mechanism is not the same as being able to explain it. In Study 1, participants rated their understanding of familiar devices, attempted detailed explanations, and then rated it again; the authors reported lower self-ratings after the attempts. This pattern supports examining the gap between felt understanding and explanatory ability, but a change in ratings alone does not measure a change in actual knowledge.

**Running example:** a zipper was one of the study's test devices. We use "knowing how to operate a zipper versus explaining how it works" as an illustration, not an invented participant transcript or an item-specific statistical result. ([Methods, Section 2.1.1](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3062901/))

## Level 1: Child / Newcomer

You might know how to zip a jacket and feel sure you understand the zipper. But could you explain what makes its parts join together? Trying to explain can reveal gaps that simply using it did not reveal.

Researchers asked adults how well they understood familiar things, then asked them to explain how some of those things worked. After trying, people generally rated their understanding lower. That does not mean trying made them forget how the things worked; it means they reported feeling less sure of their understanding. ([Study 1, results and discussion](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3062901/))

**Annotation:** the new capability is distinguishing familiarity with a device from an account of its mechanism. The explanation preserves the important boundary: the measured change was in people's ratings.

**Check 1:** If you can use a zipper successfully, does that alone show you could explain how it works? Why not?

## Level 2: Teenager / Curious Beginner

**Refinement:** the zipper story suggests an idea, but we need to distinguish the observation from the proposed reason for it.

The observation was a sequence: rate understanding, attempt a detailed explanation, then rate understanding again. The authors reported that the later ratings were lower. Their interpretation is that producing a mechanism-level explanation can expose missing connections that were hidden by a general feeling of familiarity. ([Introduction and Study 1](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3062901/))

For a zipper, "pull the tab up" describes how to use it, not why the teeth join. Being asked to explain the mechanism could make someone notice that distinction. This is an illustration of the authors' account, not a record of what any particular participant said.

A lower second rating would be consistent with discovering a gap. It would not, on its own, prove the person now knows less or that the new rating is perfectly accurate. A person might also react to task difficulty or expectations; distinguishing explanations requires more evidence.

**Annotation:** the new capability is predicting an informative pattern while separating it from a claim about its cause.

**Check 2:** If someone lowers their rating after trying to explain a zipper, which changed for certain in this observation: the reported rating, their actual knowledge, or both?

## Level 3: College Undergraduate

**Refinement:** "before and after" omitted the measurement procedure. We now need to know who was measured, what the scale meant, and which question each rating answered.

Study 1 involved 16 Yale graduate students from different departments. After training on a seven-point understanding scale, participants rated 48 items. Each then explained four test devices and made further ratings. Two groups of eight received different sets of four test devices. ([Methods, Section 2.1.1](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3062901/))

The paper labels the rating occasions as follows:

| Rating | What happened before the rating | What was being rated |
|---|---|---|
| `T1` | Initial scale training and presentation of the item list | Understanding before an explanation attempt |
| `T2` | A detailed written causal explanation of each test device | Understanding after trying to explain |
| `T3` | A diagnostic question about each device's mechanism | Understanding after the diagnostic question |
| `T4` | Reading an expert description | Reassessment of prior understanding relative to that description |
| `T5` | Reading the same expert description | Current understanding after reading it |

The distinction between `T4` and `T5` matters: they concern prior versus current understanding, not two interchangeable repetitions of the same question. ([Methods and Figure 3 caption](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3062901/))

Comparing `T1` with `T2` within participants addresses whether their reported understanding changed after the explanation attempt. It is not a comparison of a taught group with a randomized untreated group. The authors report a decrease, but these self-ratings are not an independent test score for mechanistic knowledge. ([Results, Section 2.1.2](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3062901/))

**Annotation:** the new capability is interpreting the study's measurement sequence without confusing confidence, knowledge, and the effects of reading an expert explanation.

**Check 3:** Would subtracting `T4` from `T5` isolate how much objective knowledge the expert text taught? Explain the measurement problem.

## Level 4: Graduate Student

**Refinement:** understanding the measurement sequence is not enough to evaluate or reproduce the reported result. We need the actual comparisons, materials, and competing explanations.

The authors report significant contrasts between `T1` and each of `T2`, `T3`, and `T4` at `p < .002`. The contrasts of `T2` with `T3` and of `T2` with `T4` were not significant. Thus, "every additional task caused another statistically established drop" would overstate the reported evidence. ([Results, Section 2.1.2](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3062901/))

To reproduce the study faithfully, obtain the scale-training instructions and illustrations, item lists and device-set assignments, explanation prompts, diagnostic questions, expert descriptions, and rating instructions. The paper identifies source materials and supplemental materials; they were not independently obtained for this example. A reanalysis would also require participant-level ratings and the exact aggregation and statistical procedure.

A proposed extension could randomly assign an explanation attempt versus a time-matched comparison task, and separately score mechanistic answers against a predefined rubric. That would help distinguish a specific effect of explanation attempts from effects of time, task expectations, or difficulty, while also examining actual answer quality. This is a proposed design, not a protocol or result reported for Study 1.

The authors also report higher current-understanding ratings after the expert descriptions and discuss this as evidence against a simple account of permanently depressed confidence. It does not by itself rule out every alternative explanation. ([Results and discussion, Sections 2.1.2-2.1.3](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3062901/))

**Annotation:** the new capability is outlining a verification plan and identifying the difference between reproducing the observation and discriminating among causal explanations.

**Check 4:** Why is "ratings kept falling significantly at every step" an inaccurate summary of the reported comparisons?

## Level 5: Expert

**Refinement:** the previous level considered study execution and analysis. Expert interpretation requires separating the measured result from the broader construct and proposed application.

The scoped empirical result is a within-participant shift in self-assessed mechanistic understanding under a structured sequence of tasks. The paper interprets this in terms of an illusion of explanatory depth: an inflated impression of the precision and completeness of one's explanatory knowledge. That interpretation fits the reported rating pattern, but Study 1's ratings alone do not supply an independent ground-truth scale for each participant's knowledge. ([Abstract, introduction, and Study 1](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3062901/))

Three distinctions determine what follows:

1. **Recalibration versus impairment:** lower ratings need not mean less knowledge. Establishing improved calibration would require relating self-assessment to an appropriate measure of actual explanatory performance.
2. **Observation versus causal account:** the task sequence documents a pattern; isolating the mechanism that produces it needs comparisons capable of distinguishing alternatives. The proposed extension above is one possible next step, not evidence already supplied here.
3. **Local finding versus educational prescription:** this sample and task do not establish effects for every population, subject, or teaching format. They do not establish that LLM-generated explanations produce learning, or that five levels outperform another number of levels.

The practical implication for this guide is therefore a design choice motivated by the research, not a tested consequence of the framework: ask readers to apply or explain a mechanism rather than treating a feeling of clarity as sufficient evidence of understanding. Whether that improves learning here remains an empirical question.

No priority claim about the paper's novelty is made without an adequate literature comparison.

**Annotation:** the new capability is deciding which applications the evidence licenses and what additional observations would change that assessment.

**Check 5:** What evidence would be needed to claim that this five-level guide improves learning, rather than merely making readers feel informed?
